# Settled extraction cut the read cost by a third and flipped five matched cases, none of them fresh

Completed finite experiment, 10 September 2026. Replacing one depth-first search per literal, with two freshly initialized arrays per query, by one stamped state with inheritance and settling reduced the extraction phase by 35 percent on 176 fresh cases and by 34 percent on the 162 fragment-interface-v1 cases, while deriving the same forced literals in the same order and leaving every other phase and the residual solver byte-identical. On the fresh corpus the cheaper read changed no case's standing against the baseline: both extraction arms beat the solver alone on the same 84 cases, 81 of them decided by a fragment certificate before any extraction ran. On the matched corpus it turned five losses into wins and made four chain controls 41 events worse. Every decision agreed with the independent reference, every certificate and clue path of both fragment arms passed a raw-clause checker, and no arm exhausted its budget.

The [protocol](extraction-cost-protocol.md) was fixed in commit `0e1e975` before execution. Its SHA-256, `e5da948b245df7c1055f6bdcd59e37c947f823c6ee39d858e66f3449a649f8d3`, is embedded in `src/extraction.rs` and in the artifact. The protocol also records, before any case was generated, that no linear-time read of all forced literals is known, so the two procedures share a worst-case bound and only constant factors were under test. The [full artifact](../artifacts/extraction-cost.json) contains every input with its generation parameters, the reference, all three arms with their phase counters, both fragment arms' certificates and clue paths, the settled procedure's search statistics, the checker's verdicts, and the primary and secondary summaries. Its SHA-256 is `dbc3dc212c0464a242e5555387fb1bfe102733495caa357877030b134347a3cb`.

## Reproduce the comparison

Use the repository's Rust 1.97.1 toolchain and Bun 1.3.14:

```sh
cargo run --locked --release -- extraction artifacts/extraction-cost.json
bun run check
```

The first command rewrites the artifact deterministically; `shasum -a 256 artifacts/extraction-cost.json` must print the digest above. The second command runs the repository gate: formatting, Clippy, the test suite, the ledger checks, a check that each artifact's embedded protocol digest matches its committed protocol file, and byte-for-byte replay of all six experiment artifacts.

## What was tested

Three arms decided every case with one million declared events each:

- **baseline**: the deterministic unit-propagation/DPLL solver on the original formula;
- **per-literal extraction**: the unchanged fragment-interface-v1 arm, which builds the implication graph of the binary fragment, computes its components and certificate, extracts every forced literal by one depth-first search per literal with two freshly initialized arrays, appends the units in literal order, and runs the same solver;
- **settled extraction**: the same driver with one change in the extraction phase. Four arrays are initialized once. Vertices are visited by component from sinks to sources. A vertex whose forward successor is already known to reach its own dual inherits that fact and a path without searching. Otherwise a search runs with the current stamp, stopping when it reaches the vertex's dual or an already-settled bad vertex; when a search exhausts its stack, every vertex it reached is settled as good, since any of them being bad would make the source bad.

Both fragment arms append the forced literals in the order `1, -1, 2, -2, ...`, so their processed formulas and residual solver runs are identical whenever both complete; the experiment asserts that identity, together with identical construction, components, certificate, and append counters, on every case. The difference between the two arms is therefore extraction cost alone. A separate raw-clause checker validated all 352 primary and 324 secondary certificates and all 798 primary and 410 secondary clue paths, for 556,041 events outside the arms. An independent truth-table enumeration labeled every case, for 13,526,530 events. No truth table, label, or backbone reached any arm.

The primary corpus is the protocol's 176 fresh cases: 144 random mixed 2/3-CNFs from base seeds 4001, 8009, 16001, and 32003 crossed with 8, 10, or 12 variables, ternary densities 3, 4, and 5, and binary counts n, 2n, 3n, and 4n; 24 fragment-only 2-CNFs with binary counts n and 2n; and 8 ladder controls in which a chain forces one literal and a chain of implications carries that force through j further variables. The secondary corpus is the fragment-interface-v1 corpus regenerated by its frozen generator, and the per-literal arm reproduced that experiment's per-case counters exactly, which `tests/extraction.rs` checks against the earlier artifact. No primary case coincides with any earlier corpus.

The settled procedure inherited 286 results, settled 177 vertices as good without a search, and visited 5,245 vertices in 1,361 searches. Extraction fell on all 92 cases where it ran, from 243,676 to 158,478 events, and was tied on the 84 fragment-unsatisfiable cases where neither procedure ran. ### A pre-run estimate

Before this protocol was frozen, a review discussion of a related condensation-level design estimated, from a simulation on the fragment-interface-v1 artifact, extraction at about 0.64 of the per-literal cost, five case-level flips against the baseline, and an aggregate delta still negative. That estimate was not recorded in this repository or its ledger and concerned a different procedure; it is reported as context only. The settled procedure measured 0.665, five flips, and a negative aggregate on that corpus.

## What the work counter means

Every work unit increments one checked counter: a formula check, clause or container read, literal or scalar read, clause or container write, literal or scalar write, assignment, pair check, rule attempt, or search-node visit. The settled procedure charges its four arrays and the position array once each, one container write and 2n scalar writes per array; one status read per visited vertex; the adjacency reads of an inheritance scan; the stamp, predecessor, and clause writes of every search push; one search-node event per newly stamped vertex; the reads and writes of settling a visited list; the copies of every constructed path; and the reads and writes of the simplification scan. Charges the protocol left implicit, such as grouping vertices by component and clearing the visited list, are charged and listed in the implementation. The per-literal procedure's charges are unchanged from the fragment-interface experiment.

Both procedures have worst-case cost O(n(n + m₂)) events on a fragment with n variables and m₂ binary clauses: at most 2n searches, each visiting at most 2n vertices and 2m₂ edges. The settled procedure adds O(n) initialization and output work and may run fewer than 2n searches. No linear bound holds for either, and none is claimed. The measured totals on at most 12 variables neither prove nor test the bound.

Focused correctness tests in `tests/extraction.rs` are implementation checks distinct from the reported cases: both procedures on all 512 subsets of the nine canonical width-at-most-two clauses on two variables and on each subset combined with each of the eight ternary clauses on three variables, requiring identical unit sets, checker-valid paths, and agreement with the truth table; the F_k chains for k from 2 to 11; odd and even parity cycles; the ladder controls; empty and malformed inputs; budget exhaustion at each phase boundary of the settled arm; and the per-case reproduction of the fragment-interface-v1 artifact by the per-literal arm.

## What was learned

**A third of the read cost was initialization and repeated work.** Per-literal extraction pays two fresh arrays of 2n cells for each of 2n queries; on an empty fragment that is the whole extraction phase, and the settled procedure halved it by paying once. On fragments with structure the saving came from three sources the protocol named in advance: 286 inherited answers and 177 settled vertices on the fresh corpus against 1,361 searches, and 119 and 122 against 2,587 on the matched corpus. Ladders inherited most; the v1 chain controls inherited nothing, because their forced literal's negation is the source end of the chain and is searched before any successor is known bad, and there the stamped search's extra per-visit charge made the settled arm 41 events dearer.

**Cheaper reading did not change what the fresh corpus decided.** Every win against the baseline on the fresh corpus belonged to both extraction arms. The fragment certificate decided 81 of the 84 winning cases before extraction ran, and the three wins from forced literals had margins large enough that the per-literal cost did not matter. On the 92 losing cases the settled arm remained at least 221 events above the baseline. The fresh corpus, built to have larger fragments, mostly produced unsatisfiable fragments at 3n and 4n binary clauses; that is where the interface wins, and it wins by certificate.

**The matched corpus shows where the constant matters.** Five 12-variable cases lay within about a third of their extraction cost of the baseline and crossed over. They carry between one and five forced literals. The gain is real but narrow: the settled arm's aggregate on that corpus is still about 21 percent above the baseline, and its wins rose from 22 to 27 of 162.

**What the ladders show.** A ladder plants j forced literals whose explanations grow in length; both procedures found all of them, and inheritance answered up to 10 of 24 queries. Even so, the settled arm cost about seven times the baseline on every ladder, because the baseline propagates a 2-CNF to its answer in a few hundred events. Forced literals do not repay their extraction when the formula they are extracted from is one the solver already handles by propagation.

**For the interface delta.** The [framework](../docs/knowledge-and-complexity.md#interfaces-free-lunches-and-the-cost-of-reading-a-clue) scores a representation by baseline work minus build, read, and residual work. This experiment reduced the read term by a third under a worst-case bound that cannot be improved to linear without a breakthrough, and the sign of the delta moved on five matched cases and no fresh ones. The remaining lever named by the previous report, an interface whose build cost the solver has already paid, is untouched by this result.

## Limitations

This is a calibration of two complete extraction procedures for a known interface, feeding a toy DPLL; it measures no novel inference rule, no evolutionary advantage, and nothing about general SAT complexity or P versus NP. The corpora are finite and constructed, limited to 12 declared variables and clause width three so the truth-table reference stays feasible; the pseudorandom cases are reproducible samples, not independent or representative population estimates, and no size is scaling evidence. The secondary corpus was known before this protocol was written, which is why it is reported as a matched comparison and not as the primary result. Event counts are declared operational measurements under one implementation and one cost model, not elapsed time, memory, or a proved bit-cost bound; both procedures' stated bound is a design argument the measurements do not prove. The settled procedure is a simpler scheme than the DualDFS algorithm of Buss, Kullmann, and Vassilevska Williams and inherits none of its analysis. A fragment SAT certificate is an assignment of the fragment only. Budget exhaustion would have meant unknown; none occurred.

## What this changes about the next experiment

Reading forced literals from a fragment the solver does not otherwise build has now been made as cheap as one stamped search scheme allows, and it decides the delta on only a handful of cases. The next protocol should measure the other term: an interface the residual solver already pays for. A solver that maintains its own watch lists or implication structure has charged construction; the question is the marginal read cost of forced literals from that structure and whether that read repays itself where a separately built graph did not. That experiment needs a fresh frozen protocol, and this artifact and the five earlier ones stay unchanged as the record of what a cheaper separate read buys.

- [Buss, Kullmann, and Vassilevska Williams, Dual Depth First Search for Binary Clause Reasoning (2024)](https://mathweb.ucsd.edu/~sbuss/ResearchWeb/DualDFS/DualDFS_SAT.pdf): the complete 2-CNF backbone problem, its reduction from k-cycle detection, and the failed-literal characterization of forced literals; the settled procedure here is a simpler scheme than their DualDFS and inherits none of its analysis.
- [Heule, Järvisalo, and Biere, Efficient CNF Simplification Based on Binary Implication Graphs (2011)](https://www.cs.utexas.edu/~marijn/publications/unhiding.pdf): failed literals on the binary implication graph and the conjecture that their fixpoint computation is at least quadratic.
